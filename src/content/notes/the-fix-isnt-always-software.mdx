When a business asks for help, the request often arrives already shaped as a product: *we need an app*, *we need a dashboard*, *we need to automate this*. Sometimes that's right. But the research on operational problems keeps pointing at something less exciting: a lot of the biggest wins come from changing how existing things are used.

This note collects three well-documented examples. We didn't run these studies — they're published work — but they shape how we start every engagement.

## 1. The traffic light that was never retimed

Signal timing plans are set once and then drift out of date as traffic changes. The US Federal Highway Administration's guidance on arterial management summarises decades of evaluations of simply **re-timing existing signals**: one state programme measured about 12–13% less delay and 11–12% fewer stops, and benefit–cost ratios in the tens are commonly reported. Agencies are advised to revisit timings every one to three years.

No new hardware. No new software for drivers. Someone counted the traffic, worked out what the green time should be, and loaded a new plan.

*Source: [FHWA, Arterial Management Program](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop09006/app_c.htm).*

## 2. The beds that free up at 4 pm

Emergency departments get blocked when patients who need a ward bed can't get one. Often the beds exist — they just become free late in the afternoon, because that's when discharges happen.

A hospital programme reported in *BMC Health Services Research* (2024) raised the share of discharges completed before noon from 11% to 38%, and kept it there for 41 months, mostly through planning: deciding the day before, getting medicines and paperwork ready in the morning, and giving patients somewhere to wait for their ride. Simulation studies suggest earlier discharges cut emergency-department boarding substantially, though one large observational study found no clear effect — so the result depends on the hospital.

*Sources: [BMC Health Services Research, 2024](https://bmchealthservres.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12913-024-10960-x.pdf); [simulation of discharge timing (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20888163).*

## 3. The air that leaks away overnight

Compressed air is one of the most expensive utilities in a factory, and leaks are routine. The Compressed Air Challenge puts typical leakage at 20–30% of compressor output in plants without a leak programme; guidance linked to the US Department of Energy says a managed system can hold it under 10%.

The fix is a person with an ultrasonic detector, a roll of tags, and a repair list — repeated regularly. A flow meter helps you notice when it creeps back.

*Sources: [Compressed Air Challenge, via Fluid Power World](https://www.fluidpowerworld.com/?p=140736); [US DOE / NREL leak guidance](https://nrel.gov/docs/fy01osti/28008.pdf).*

## What the three have in common

- **The problem was measured before anything was changed.** Turning counts, discharge times by hour, leaks found and tagged.
- **The cause wasn't where people first looked.** Not "too many cars", "too few beds" or "we need bigger compressors".
- **The fix was mostly process, and cheap.** Software can help sustain it, but it wasn't the starting point.

## Why this shapes how we work

It's why every engagement we do starts on site, with measurement, before we propose anything. Sometimes the answer will be software — we build a lot of it, and our own projects are software. But if a retimed schedule or a better handoff does the job, that's what we'll recommend, and we'll show you the numbers before and after.
